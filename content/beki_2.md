# Beki embankments
The Beki embankments were primarily constructed during the 1960s and 1970s as part of broader flood-control initiatives undertaken across Assam. The principal objective behind these embankments was to protect agricultural lands, settlements, roads, and public infrastructure from annual floods and riverbank erosion. At that time, embankments were viewed as symbols of development and security. For many communities living close to the river, they represented state intervention and the promise of stability in an otherwise uncertain flood-prone landscape.
In the initial years, the embankments provided temporary relief from seasonal inundation. Agricultural production increased in certain areas due to reduced flood exposure, and some settlements expanded in locations previously considered vulnerable. However, over time, several unintended ecological and social consequences began to emerge. The Beki, like many Himalayan-fed rivers, carries enormous volumes of sediment from the upstream catchments in Bhutan and the eastern Himalayas. Once confined within embankments, the river gradually lost space to spread and deposit sediments naturally across the floodplain. As a result, sediment started accumulating within the river channel itself, raising the riverbed and increasing pressure on embankment walls.
Today, many stretches of the Beki embankments are in fragile condition. Frequent breaches during high rainfall years and sudden water releases from upstream regions have become common concerns for local communities. Instead of preventing disasters entirely, embankments often intensify flooding when breaches occur, causing sudden and destructive inundation in downstream villages. In many places, erosion has continued despite embankment construction, while new erosion zones have emerged near the edges and downstream stretches.
The embankments have also altered the ecological character of the river basin. Traditional wetlands, side channels, and seasonal floodplains that once absorbed excess water have either disappeared or become disconnected from the river. Fish migration patterns have been disrupted, and local biodiversity has declined in several stretches. Communities that historically adapted to seasonal flooding through elevated housing, crop diversity, and mobility now find themselves increasingly dependent on hard infrastructure that often fails during extreme climatic events.
At present, there is growing recognition among researchers, policymakers, and local communities that embankments alone cannot solve the complex challenges of the Beki river basin. Discussions are increasingly focusing on integrated river basin management approaches that combine ecological restoration, community participation, wetland conservation, erosion-sensitive planning, and adaptive flood governance. The future of the Beki basin may therefore depend not only on strengthening embankments physically, but also on rethinking how rivers are governed and how people coexist with dynamic floodplain environments.
